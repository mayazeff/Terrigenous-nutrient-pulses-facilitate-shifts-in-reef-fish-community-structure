# Pulsed resource subsidies restructure reef fish communities through behavioral plasticity 


Zeff, M.<sup>1*</sup>, Barnas, D. M.<sup>2</sup>, Silbiger, N. J.<sup>3</sup>

1. Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology, University of California Santa Cruz, Santa Cruz, CA, United States
2. University of Hawai'i at Mānoa, Hawai'i Institute of Marine Biology, Kāne'ohe, HI, United States
3. University of Hawai'i at Mānoa, Uehiro Center for the Advancement of Oceanography, Department of Oceanography, Honolulu, HI, United States

\*Correspondence: mayazeff@ucsc.edu

**Keywords:** resource pulse, trophic plasticity, nutrient subsidy, submarine groundwater discharge, herbivory, omnivory, coral reef, community structure, reef fish


**Abstract:** Episodic surges in resource availability structure communities from temperate forests to tropical rivers, triggering consumer responses that cascade through food webs. Resource pulse theory predicts diet switching as a key exploitation strategy, yet most documented responses involve aggregation and elevated consumption, leaving the role of rapid dietary shifts largely untested. Submarine groundwater discharge (SGD) provides a model system for examining this mechanism, as tidal forcing creates predictable nutrient pulses through discharge peaks during low tide. We investigated how tidally pulsed nutrients from SGD affect reef fish community structure, feeding behavior, and herbivory across SGD-influenced and ambient environments in a tropical coral reef lagoon. Using a combination of video surveys, herbivory assays, and nutrient analyses, we found that SGD drives multi-scale ecological changes including shifts in individual feeding behaviors, community restructuring, and the coupling of bottom-up nutrient enrichment with top-down consumer control. SGD environments supported 56% higher species richness during peak discharge periods and harbored distinct assemblages dominated by omnivorous and herbivorous fishes, including resident populations of euryhaline species. Omnivorous fishes exhibited pronounced dietary plasticity exclusively in SGD environments, shifting from benthic to water column feeding during low-tide discharge pulses when productivity peaked. Two independent mechanisms drove elevated herbivory: herbivores consumed SGD-collected algae at 3-fold higher rates, likely reflecting elevated nitrogen content, and consumed algae at 6-fold higher rates in SGD environments regardless of algal source. Together, our results demonstrate that consumers can track resource pulses through rapid diet switching, coupling environmental variability to ecosystem processes through (1) temporal variation in resource use that enhances diversity, (2) dietary plasticity linking nutrient pulses to water column exploitation, and (3) additive effects of bottom-up enrichment and top-down intensification of herbivory.
