# Pulsed resource subsidies restructure reef fish communities through behavioral plasticity 


Zeff, M.<sup>1*</sup>, Barnas, D. M.<sup>2</sup>, Silbiger, N. J.<sup>3</sup>

1. Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology, University of California Santa Cruz, Santa Cruz, CA, United States
2. University of Hawai'i at Mānoa, Hawai'i Institute of Marine Biology, Kāne'ohe, HI, United States
3. University of Hawai'i at Mānoa, Uehiro Center for the Advancement of Oceanography, Department of Oceanography, Honolulu, HI, United States

\*Correspondence: mayazeff@ucsc.edu

**Keywords:** submarine groundwater discharge, coral reefs, herbivory, feeding behavior, environmental gradients, Moorea, omnivory


**Abstract:** Coral reefs support high biodiversity and productivity, yet occur in waters that are often thought to be oligotrophic. Submarine groundwater discharge (SGD) may help resolve this paradox by delivering nutrient-rich terrigenous freshwater in tidally modulated pulses that create temporally variable resource subsidies. We examined how SGD affects reef fish community structure, feeding behavior, and herbivory across SGD-influenced and ambient sites in a tropical coral reef lagoon, using video surveys, herbivory assays, and nutrient analyses. SGD drove multi-scale ecological change, from individual feeding behavior to community restructuring, and coupled bottom-up nutrient enrichment with top-down consumer control. SGD sites supported 58% higher species richness during peak discharge and harbored distinct assemblages characterized by elevated abundances of omnivorous and herbivorous fishes, including euryhaline species absent from nearby long-term monitoring datasets. Omnivores showed pronounced dietary plasticity exclusively at SGD sites, shifting toward water-column feeding during low-tide discharge pulses. Herbivory intensified through two independent mechanisms: algae from SGD sites were consumed at 3-fold higher rates, likely due to higher percent nitrogen content, while fishes feeding at SGD sites grazed at 6-fold higher rates regardless of algal source. Together, these results demonstrate that SGD functions as a critical but under-appreciated driver of coral reef structure and function, generating (1) distinct assemblages, (2) temporal shifts in feeding strategies, and (3) coupled nutrient enrichment and enhanced herbivory. The prevalence of SGD in tropical coastal systems globally indicates these ecological patterns may be widespread, with important implications for how localized resource subsidies maintain coral reef biodiversity and function.
